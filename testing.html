Identity comes first

Mass spectrometry ionises the sample and measures mass-to-charge ratio. The observed mass either matches the expected mass for the sequence or it does not. This is the test that answers "is this the molecule on the label."

It gets skipped more often than it should, because purity is the number people look for. But purity is a ratio of one peak to all peaks — it describes how dominant the main species is, not what that species is. A sample can be 99% one compound and that compound can be the wrong one.

Purity is a method, not a number

Reversed-phase HPLC pushes the sample through a packed column under pressure. Components that interact more strongly with the stationary phase come out later. A detector at the far end records absorbance, and each species draws a peak.

Area percent is the area under the main peak divided by the total area under all peaks. Change the wavelength, the gradient, or the column and the same vial gives a different figure — which is why we publish the method and the detection wavelength next to the percentage. A purity number with no method attached cannot be compared to anything.

There is a second trap. Area percent, net peptide content, and total weight purity are three different measurements that all get printed as a percentage. A vial can read 99% by HPLC and still be a substantial fraction salt and residual water by mass, because HPLC only sees what absorbs at that wavelength. Net peptide content is the figure that tells you how much peptide you actually bought.

Blends need a fourth test

When three compounds share a vial, purity tells you how clean the contents are and identity tells you the components are the right ones. Neither tells you the proportion.

Fill weight is not proof of ratio. Two powders can be weighed correctly and still separate, settle, or partition unevenly during lyophilisation. Ratio verification runs the reconstituted blend through HPLC and integrates each component peak separately, so the stated split is measured rather than assumed.

Every blend in the register — Wolverine, Deadpool, Glow, and Klow — carries one.

Lyophilisation, and the clock it stops

Freeze-drying removes water by sublimation: the sample is frozen, then held under vacuum so ice passes straight to vapour without melting. Skipping the liquid phase is the point — liquid water drives hydrolysis, and hydrolysis is what breaks peptide bonds.

The result is a dry cake that is stable at room temperature in transit and keeps far longer frozen. Adding diluent restarts the clock the dry state had stopped. After that: 2–8 °C, out of the light, and as few freeze–thaw cycles as the work allows.
